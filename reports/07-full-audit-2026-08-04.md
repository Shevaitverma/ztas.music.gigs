# 1. Executive Summary

**Verdict: this is a well-engineered chassis with no engine, no fuel line, and a moat that no user can currently reach. It cannot become a business in its present state, but the distance to a testable business is measured in weeks, not quarters — provided the founder stops building and starts wiring.**

Six weeks ago a prior audit concluded "the register isn't installed." That conclusion is still true today, and **almost nothing has changed since**: the last commit is 2026-07-01. What did land in that commit was real and good — phone OTP login, the review artist-resolution fix, gig visibility hardening, WS bid-room access control, an honest rewrite of the landing page. What did not land is the entire reason the product exists.

Six findings dominate everything else in this report. All six are new to this audit, and the verification pass made four of them worse than my first draft stated:

**1. The OTP check-in moat is not merely unbuilt on the frontend — it is unreachable, and the path that replaced it defeats its own purpose.** `web/lib/api/checkin.ts` exists and is **imported by zero pages** (verified). It is also *incomplete*: it has no `startEvent` call, so the server's `CHECKED_IN → EVENT_STARTED → EVENT_ENDED` machine can never advance even if a UI existed; and its `verifyOtp` omits the `location` object the server requires (`checkin.schemas.ts:19`), so it would 400 on first contact. Meanwhile `POST /gigs/:id/complete` (`gigs.service.ts:429`) lets **the client unilaterally mark a gig COMPLETED with no OTP, no artist confirmation, and no counter-signature**. Because the scheduler downgrades unconfirmed booked gigs to `CLOSED` 24h post-event, the *only functioning path to COMPLETED today is the one that bypasses the moat entirely.* When escrow is wired to `COMPLETED`, that route becomes a unilateral fund-release lever held by the payer.

**2. The entire post-booking half of the product does not exist in the UI.** No check-in screen, no review-creation screen (`reviewsApi.create` is never called from any page — verified), no payment, no payout. `artist/earnings/page.tsx` ships **hardcoded zeros behind a `// Placeholder data` comment**. A user who completes the happy path — post gig, receive bids, accept one — arrives at a screen reading "Booked — event check-in & payment coming soon" and the product ends. Every downstream artefact the business depends on (reviews, ratings, GMV, repeat rate) is therefore **structurally impossible to generate**, not merely absent.

**3. There is no notification delivery of any kind.** No FCM, no SMS, no email, no WhatsApp — verified by exhaustive grep. `Notification` rows are written to Mongo and never leave it. In a reverse auction, "you have been outbid" is the entire engagement loop. Without delivery, the loop is: log in, refresh, hope. This alone would kill retention on a product whose other organs worked.

**4. The admin verification console does not work at all — so KYC documents can be collected but never processed.** This corrects a claim I made in my first draft on the strength of `PROJECT_CONTEXT.md`, which describes three admin surfaces "wired end-to-end." Two independent contract breaks kill the KYC queue: the admin sends `status: 'pending'` (`admin/lib/api/verifications.ts:41`) while the server enum is `'PENDING'` and applies the filter raw (`verification.service.ts:264`), so **the queue matches nothing and reads "Inbox zero" forever**; and the admin invents a discriminant `kind` where the server returns `type` (`admin/lib/types.ts:150` vs `verification.service.ts:557`), so **every "Review" link resolves to `/verifications/undefined/<id>` and 404s.** Compounding it, KYC approvals write **zero audit-log entries and send zero notifications** (grep count 0 in `verification.service.ts`) while the UI tells the admin "The submitter is notified on rejection." The practical consequence is severe: the platform is *already live and collecting* Aadhaar, PAN, GST, bank account, IFSC, and selfie-with-ID uploads that **no human being can review.** Dual-side KYC is the one asset I rated as a genuine competitive advantage; it is currently a liability-accumulating write-only store.

**5. Fabricated trust signals are still live inside the product.** The landing page was cleaned of fake ratings in June — the verification pass confirms all four June issues genuinely fixed — but **the app itself was not.** Hardcoded literals render as if fetched: every artist bidding on your gig shows `4.9` (`client/gigs/[id]/page.tsx:328`); every artist card shows `4.8` and "12 Gigs Done" (`client/artists/page.tsx:208`, `[id]/page.tsx:105,124`); every client shows `4.8` / "12 gigs posted" (`artist/gigs/[id]/page.tsx:310-311`); and a brand-new artist sees themselves rated **"4.9 (28 reviews)"** (`artist/profile/page.tsx:206`). Clients choose artists on a number that is a literal. Combined with finding 2 — no review can ever be created — the marketplace's entire trust surface is fiction, and unlike the landing page it sits *inside* the transaction flow.

**6. 42 dependency vulnerabilities, 4 critical and 19 high** (`bun audit`, server, deps installed). The HTTP front door itself is affected: the installed `elysia` resolves below the patched 1.4.26 and carries a **remotely-triggerable, unauthenticated URL-format ReDoS** (GHSA-f45g-68q3-5w8x). `mongoose ^9.0.1` fails to sanitize `$nor` → NoSQL injection (GHSA-wpg9-53fq-2r8h), directly relevant because `admin.service.ts:293-355` builds `$or` filters from user input. Transitively via `firebase-admin`: `protobufjs` **arbitrary code execution**, `fast-xml-parser` 2× critical, `node-forge` RSA/Ed25519 signature forgery. `bun update` clears the direct deps within semver; the firebase chain needs overrides. **My first draft marked OWASP A06 "not assessed" — this is the answer, and it is the single cheapest Critical to close in the entire report.**

**What is genuinely good, with evidence:** typecheck passes clean; 21 tests green; the auth stack is better than most seed-stage products I audit (httpOnly cookies, DB-sourced roles per request at `app.ts:126` so bans take effect immediately, refresh tokens stored SHA-256-hashed with `timingSafeEqual`, ws-tickets at 30s TTL instead of tokens-in-URLs, socket-peer-derived rate limiting that can't be forged via `X-Forwarded-For`); PII is AES-256-GCM encrypted with field-bound AAD; the bid-accept path is genuinely concurrency-aware; the dependency footprint is lean and current (15 prod deps in web, no bloat). **The landing page has been honestly rewritten** — the fabricated `aggregateRating`, fake testimonials, and USD tiers flagged in June are gone, replaced with INR pricing, explicit beta disclosure, and a comment in `layout.tsx:96` explaining *why* there's no review schema. That is a founder who took the feedback seriously.

**The strategic problem is separate from and larger than the engineering problem.** The pricing primitive is a strict descending reverse auction: every new bid must undercut the current lowest (`bids.service.ts:100–114`), and artists may only *lower* an existing bid, never raise it (`159–163`). This is not "bidding" — it is an enforced race to the bottom with a ratchet. Good musicians do not participate in these. The two dead comparables in this exact space (Sonicbids, Elance) used exactly this model. The prior research (report 04, ~130 sources, **not independently verified by me**) reaches the same conclusion from market evidence; I reach it from the code.

**Bottom line:** the fastest path to a revenue-generating launch is not more features. It is (a) wire the check-in and review UI that the backend already supports, (b) close the `POST /gigs/:id/complete` bypass, (c) install commission-from-escrow on the OTP trigger, (d) ship *any* notification channel, and (e) replace the auction with quote-compare. That is roughly **12–16 dev-weeks to a monetizable v1**. Everything else in this report is subordinate to those five items.

**Overall Business Score: 4.4 / 10** — a strong 7/10 codebase attached to a 2/10 revenue apparatus and a 3/10 pricing model. Detailed scorecard in §29.

# 2. Product Overview

## What it does (FACT, High confidence)

A two-sided India-focused marketplace where **event organisers ("clients")** post music gigs with a budget range, and **musicians ("artists")** compete for them via a descending reverse auction. An admin tier moderates KYC verification, user reports, and account status.

## Who it is for

- **Primary supply:** Indian gigging musicians — the code's enums cover Bollywood, Sufi, Ghazal, Classical; `PerformanceType`, `instruments`, `genres`, `languages`, `baseRate`, geo-point on `artistProfile`.
- **Primary demand:** Event organisers. Schema signals point overwhelmingly at **weddings and private/corporate events** (venue + date + start/end time + equipment-provided + budget range), not ticketed live music.
- **Geography:** India-only, structurally. Aadhaar/PAN/GST fields, IFSC/UPI payout fields, INR hardcoded in error strings (`bids.service.ts:80`), Mumbai sample data.

## The problem it claims to solve

Booking a musician for an event in India is an opaque, broker-mediated, trust-poor transaction. Organisers cannot verify an artist is real, will show up, or is priced fairly. Artists cannot verify the organiser will pay. Both sides currently route through agencies taking undisclosed markups, or through WhatsApp referral chains with no recourse.

# 3. Business Evaluation

## Revenue model

**FACT (High):** There is none, in code. Exhaustive grep for `razorpay|escrow|payout|commission|cashfree|stripe` across all four apps returns **three incidental hits**, none of them a payment implementation. There is no `Transaction` model. `PROJECT_CONTEXT.md` documents a `PENDING_PAYMENT → ESCROW → RELEASED/DISPUTED/REFUNDED` state machine that **does not exist in the codebase**. The admin revenue endpoint returns a hardcoded zero.

**The intended model** (from the landing page, which is now honest about it): free to join; ~10–12% commission on completed bookings; escrow with OTP-gated release, "rolling out during the beta."

### Is ~10–12% the right number?

**Recommendation: yes, flat, with a floor fee. Do not tier it upward on small gigs.**

- **Why:** Prior research (report 04, ASSUMPTION — sources not re-verified by me) puts survivors at 5–20%: Poptop ~12%, Encore ~20%, GigSalad ~2.5–5% + client fee, StarClinch est. 15–20%. The previously-planned *15% under ₹10k → 8% above ₹100k* tier is backwards. A 15% cut on an ₹8,000 wedding-DJ booking is ₹1,200 — precisely the amount that makes both parties say "let's just do this on UPI," and they already have each other's phone numbers (§2). High take rates on low-value transactions maximise leakage exactly where enforcement is weakest.
- **Business impact:** At flat 12%, GMV of ₹1 crore/month → ₹12 lakh/month gross revenue, less ~2% PG fees and 18% GST on commission. Net ~₹9.6 lakh.
- **Technical impact:** A flat rate is one constant. The tiered version needs a rate table, effective-dating, and grandfathering. Flat is strictly cheaper to build and to explain.
- **Cost:** ~0 (it's a config value inside work already scoped in §17).
- **Difficulty:** Trivial. **Priority: High** (decide before building, not after). **ROI: high** — avoids a rebuild and avoids the leakage cliff.

### Secondary revenue — realistic ordering

1. **"Verified Pro" supply-side subscription** (₹499–999/mo): profile badge, priority in search ranking, more audio samples. Buildable in ~2 dev-weeks *on top of KYC that already exists*. **This is the fastest real rupee in the codebase** — it needs no payment escrow, just a recurring charge. **Recommend shipping this before commission.**
2. **Featured gig placement** for clients (₹500–2,000/post). ~1 dev-week.
3. **Cross-pitching / ensemble seats** — artist-to-artist bookings, turning supply into demand. Strategically the strongest idea in the prior reports, but it is a *frequency* play that only pays off after the money layer works. **Defer.**
4. **API / white-label** — no market evidence, no inbound demand, and no API-consumer story. **Reject for now.**

## Unit economics (ASSUMPTION-heavy, Low–Medium confidence — no real data exists)

Modelled at flat 12%, average booking ₹25,000 (mid-point of the DJ ₹25k–₹2L band cited in prior research):

| Metric | Value | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue per completed booking | ₹3,000 | 12% of ₹25,000 |
| Less PG fee (~2% of GMV) | −₹500 | Razorpay Route standard |
| Less GST on commission (18%) | −₹540 | On commission only |
| **Contribution per booking** | **~₹1,960** | |
| Bookings/client/year | **1.2** | ASSUMPTION — weddings are ~once-per-lifetime; corporate/private repeat is the only source of >1 |
| **Client LTV** | **~₹2,350** | Brutally low |
| Bookings/artist/year | 6–12 | ASSUMPTION — artists are the repeat side |
| **Artist LTV (as supply)** | **₹11,800–23,500** | |

**The decisive economic insight, and it is not in the prior reports:** at a client LTV of ~₹2,350, **paid client acquisition is structurally impossible.** Indian wedding-vertical CAC via Google/Meta runs ₹800–3,000 for a *lead*, not a booking; at any plausible lead→booking conversion the CAC exceeds LTV several times over. **This business can only work if client acquisition is ~free (SEO, artist-driven referral, marketplace network effects) and if artists are the monetised, retained side.** Every growth recommendation in §19 follows from this constraint, and it argues strongly for making the artist subscription — not the client-side commission — the strategic centre of the model.

## Margins

Gross margin on commission is ~65% after PG + GST (High confidence on the arithmetic; Low on the inputs). That is healthy. The threat to margin is not COGS — it is **support cost per dispute**, which is unmodelled and, for wedding events, potentially severe. A disputed ₹25,000 booking that consumes two hours of founder time has already destroyed 2× its contribution margin.

# 4. Market Analysis

*Sourcing note: the figures below are from `reports/04-business-model-research.md` (~130 cited sources, 2026-06-26). I read that report but did **not** re-verify its sources in this pass. Treat as **ASSUMPTION, Medium confidence**. My own contribution here is the interpretation and the TAM/SAM/SOM derivation.*

## Industry & size

- Indian weddings: **~US$130B/year** (Jefferies 2024), 8–10M weddings/year, average spend ₹39.5 lakh.
- Entertainment ≈ **5–10% of wedding budget** → wedding entertainment **≈ US$6.5B** at the conservative end.
- Organised live events: **₹13,000 crore (~US$1.55B) in 2025, +44% YoY** — fastest-growing M&E segment (EY/BookMyShow).
- Events & exhibitions: US$5.69B (2025) → US$9.04B (2031), ~8% CAGR.
- UPI: 23.2B transactions/month — payment rails are turnkey and near-free.

## TAM / SAM / SOM (my derivation, Medium–Low confidence)

| | Definition | Value |
|---|---|---|
| **TAM** | All money paid to music performers for events in India | **~US$6.5B** (~₹54,000 cr) |
| **SAM** | Urban, digitally-mediated, formal-payment bookings in top-8 metros | **~₹5,400 cr** (assume 10% of TAM is addressable by an app today) |
| **SOM (3yr, realistic)** | 0.2–0.5% of SAM | **₹11–27 cr GMV/yr → ₹1.3–3.2 cr revenue/yr at 12%** |

**Reality check on SOM:** StarClinch — founded 2015, 18–30k artists, self-described category leader — does **~₹2.4 crore revenue (FY25)** on only $233K raised. That is the honest ceiling for an unfunded India artist marketplace over a decade. **The market is large; the *capturable, commission-bearing* slice has repeatedly proven small.** Anyone modelling this business at ₹50 cr revenue in year three is modelling a different company than the one the comparables describe.

## Timing, crowding, and the actual whitespace

- **Growing:** yes, strongly (44% YoY in live events).
- **Crowded:** yes at the *discovery* layer (WedMeGood 5.5M monthly visitors, Sulekha, JustDial, Instagram, agencies).
- **Underserved:** yes at the *transaction* layer. WedMeGood explicitly does not process bookings and does not guarantee leads; it sells ₹50k–₹85k visibility packages and lets the deal close off-platform. StarClinch transacts but is undercapitalised.
- **Timing verdict:** **Good, with a caveat.** UPI + Razorpay Route make escrow cheap in a way it wasn't in 2015. But the reason incumbents stay small is *not* that they lacked escrow — it is that **high-value, low-frequency, relationship-driven bookings leak off-platform structurally.** ZTS has no answer to this yet. Its stated answer (escrow) is unbuilt, and its current behaviour (handing over phone numbers at acceptance) is the opposite of an answer.

## Threats

| Threat | Likelihood | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| WedMeGood adds transactions (has traffic + capital + vendor base) | **Medium-High** | **Severe** — they already own discovery |
| Instagram/WhatsApp remains "good enough" for direct booking | **High** | **Severe** — it is the actual incumbent |
| A funded entrant copies the model | Low-Medium | Moderate |
| Payment-aggregator regulatory change | Low | Moderate |

**The real competitor is not StarClinch. It is a WhatsApp group and a UPI QR code.** Any strategy that does not explain why an organiser and artist who already have each other's numbers would route ₹25,000 through a platform that charges 12% is not a strategy.

# 5. Customer Analysis

## Personas

**Organiser — "Priya", 29, Mumbai, booking her sangeet.** Buys once in a lifetime. Zero brand loyalty possible. Budget ₹50k–₹2L for entertainment. JTBD: *"Help me hire a band that will definitely show up and definitely be good, without me becoming an expert in booking bands."* Pains: no way to verify quality; terrified of a no-show on an unrepeatable day; suspects agency markup. **Will pay for:** certainty and recourse. **Will not pay for:** discovery — Instagram is free. **Retention potential: near zero.** Treat as one-shot.

**Organiser — "Rohit", 38, corporate events / venue manager.** Books 10–40 acts/year. **This is the only genuinely retainable demand persona in the market, and the product is not designed for him.** He needs saved rosters, repeat booking, invoicing, GST-compliant receipts, and a PO-friendly payment flow. He has none of these. JTBD: *"Reliably fill my calendar with vetted acts without doing 40 separate negotiations."* **Recommendation: this persona should be the ICP.** He alone makes CAC recoverable (§3).

**Artist — "Arjun", 26, wedding-circuit keyboardist.** Plays 40–80 gigs/year. JTBD: *"Fill my empty weekends and stop being underpaid by agents."* Pains: income volatility, agency cuts, chasing payment. **Will pay for:** deal flow — this is the classic supply-side willingness-to-pay that makes marketplace subscriptions work. **Will NOT accept:** a public descending auction that broadcasts his floor price to every competitor and every future client. **Retention potential: high.** He is the repeat side, the monetisable side, and the referral engine.

**Artist — "Meera", 34, established vocalist with an agent.** The supply that makes the marketplace credible. **Will never use a reverse auction.** She sets a rate; she does not bid down against unknowns. **Currently structurally excluded by the pricing primitive.** Every quality-supply strategy requires killing the auction first.

## Decision makers vs. buyers

For weddings, the payer (parents) and the chooser (couple) frequently differ, and neither is the coordinator (wedding planner). **Wedding planners are an unexploited channel:** one planner books dozens of acts a year and is an aggregator of exactly the repeat demand that Priya cannot provide. The product has no planner/agency account type. **This is the single largest missing customer segment.**

## Would they switch, and why not?

**Would switch for:** verified artists with real reviews (once reviews exist), escrow protection (once it exists), a no-show guarantee (does not exist and is not planned).
**Would not switch because:** their cousin knows a guy; Instagram is free; 12% on ₹25,000 is real money; and — decisively — **the platform currently offers nothing after the booking, so there is nothing to switch *to*.** A user who tries ZTS today gets a slightly nicer way to find someone's phone number.

## Why a user would choose ZTS

1. Escrow + OTP release — *the strongest differentiator, and it does not work yet.*

# 9. Technical Audit

## Overall engineering quality: **7/10** — genuinely above average for this stage

Modern stack (Bun + Elysia + Mongoose 9 + Next 16 + React 19), consistent module structure (`routes/service/schemas/gateway`), clean typecheck, lean dependencies, and — unusually — **the security comments in the code are accurate**, which is rare and indicates real review discipline rather than cargo-culted hardening.

## Architecture

**Good:** clear plugin composition with documented ordering (`app.ts:33–80`); consistent `routes → service → model` layering; shared utils properly factored; Elysia plugin naming to dedupe hooks.

**Concerns:**

1. **Single-instance assumptions are load-bearing and undocumented (FACT, High).**
   - Rate limiter is an in-process `Map` (`security.plugin.ts:69`). Two replicas → 2× the limit; restart → limits reset. **The 5-per-15-min login limit is not a real control in any horizontally-scaled deployment.**
   - Scheduler uses in-process `setInterval` (`scheduler.service.ts:29–50`). N replicas → N concurrent sweeps → duplicate notifications and racing status writes.
   - **This blocks horizontal scaling entirely and there is no comment anywhere acknowledging it.** Fix: Redis for rate limits, a distributed lock or external cron for the scheduler. ~1.5 dev-weeks. **Priority: High before any production traffic.**

2. **🔴 No transactions on multi-document writes — three concrete corruption windows (FACT, High).** `acceptBid` (`bids.service.ts:365–441`) does bid-claim → gig-claim → rollback-on-failure across three unordered writes. The verification pass enumerated exactly where it breaks:
   - **W1 — bid ACCEPTED, gig unbooked.** Crash after `:387`, before `:410`. The gig stays `LIVE` and keeps accepting bids, while the artist's dashboard shows it as won and counts it toward `totalEarnings`. **No self-heal exists** — `autoCompleteGigs` requires `acceptedArtist` (`:257`) so the scheduler will never touch it. Needs a DBA.
   - **W2 — two ACCEPTED bids on one gig.** `lockedGig` is null (rival won) and the process dies before the rollback at `:414-417` lands. Two artists both hold `ACCEPTED`, both believe they are booked. **This is precisely the double-booking the comment at `:347-353` claims to prevent.**
   - **W3 — orphaned siblings**, which exposed a bug I missed: **`updateBidAmount` (`:142-196`) never checks gig status.** Artists whose bids were left `PENDING` on an already-`BOOKED` gig can keep lowering them indefinitely.
   **Fix:** Atlas is a replica set, so `session.withTransaction()` around `:383-438` is available — roughly a 15-line diff that deletes the rollback branch and eliminates W2 by construction. **~2 days. This is the highest-severity data-integrity issue in the codebase.**

3. **Auth does a Mongo round-trip per authenticated request** (`app.ts:126`). *This is the correct security decision* — it makes bans effective immediately — but it is unmitigated. At 1,000 rps that is 1,000 extra queries/sec. Fix: 30-second in-process or Redis cache keyed by user ID, invalidated on ban. ~2 days. Defer until load justifies it.

RESTish and mostly consistent. Real inconsistencies found:
- `PUT /admin/users/:id/status` where every sibling uses PATCH semantics.
- Phone verify accepts the Firebase token via `X-Firebase-Token` header while Google verify uses a body field — two shapes for one operation.

## Database schema

**Good:** proper GeoJSON + 2dsphere, text index on gigs, sensible sub-documents, `select: false` on `password`/`refreshToken`.

**Problems:**
1. **`Bid` and `Application` are duplicate concepts** (`bid.model.ts` 49 LOC, `application.model.ts` 44 LOC), both live, forcing dual cross-rejection in every accept path. **Delete `Application`.**
2. **`Gig.acceptedApplicant` is deprecated but still queried** in reviews and bid-accept guards — the exact class of drift that caused the June review bug.
3. **`bidCount` is not decremented on mass-rejection** (`bids.service.ts:424–431`) while `withdrawBid` and `rejectBid` both decrement. **Counters drift upward permanently after every accept.** This is user-visible ("12 bids" on a gig with 1).

6. **🔴 CORRECTION TO MY OWN FINDING — the bid uniqueness constraint exists and is *too strict*, and it returns a 500.** My first draft said no compound-unique index prevents two `PENDING` bids. That was **wrong.** `bid.model.ts:43` declares `{gigId, artistId}` unique — but across **all** statuses, not just `PENDING`. Meanwhile `placeBid:85-89` only checks for an existing *PENDING* bid. ## Open findings

### 🔴 CRITICAL — Every bank IFSC code ever collected is silently corrupted and unrecoverable
**FACT (High). Found 2026-08-04 during Go-migration review; empirically reproduced. FIXED in code, but the existing data is gone.**

`artist-verification.model.ts` declared the field as:
```ts
ifscCode: { type: String, required: true, trim: true, uppercase: true, set: aadIfsc, get: aadIfscGet }
```
**Mongoose applies built-in transforms (`trim`/`uppercase`/`lowercase`) *after* custom `set` functions.** So the encrypt setter ran first, and `uppercase: true` then uppercased the resulting ciphertext. Reproduced against the installed Mongoose:

```
input:   "sbin0001234"
control field (no uppercase): enc:v1:8mP2MXubskqtJtoRZwhJTGlx…   ✅
ifscCode field:               ENC:V1:Q9OWHTC+YB9AMZ5N2HBVGEFO…   ❌
```

Two compounding failures:
1. **The data is destroyed.** Base64 is case-sensitive. Uppercasing the payload is irreversible — the plaintext IFSC codes are **not recoverable**. Affected records must be re-collected from users.
2. **It fails silently, and leaks.** The mangled `ENC:V1:` no longer matches the `enc:v1:` sentinel, so `decryptPii` takes its legacy-plaintext branch and **returns the corrupted string verbatim instead of raising**. Nothing ever errored.

**Scope — exactly one field.** `ifscCode` is the only encrypted field carrying `uppercase`. `gstNumber` and `panNumber` carry `trim: true`, which is a harmless no-op on base64 (no surrounding whitespace). `accountNumber` and both `identity.number` fields carry no transform.

**Fixed:** normalisation moved inside the setter (`v.trim().toUpperCase()` *before* `encryptPii`), and `trim`/`uppercase` removed from the field. A regression test (`src/test/pii-setter-order.test.ts`) pins both the hazard and the fix, and asserts the real schema declares no post-setter transforms on that path.

**Still required from you:** identify affected artist bank records and re-collect the IFSC codes. Audit any future `set:` field for co-declared transforms — this pattern is a landmine anywhere it recurs.

### 🔴 CRITICAL — The rate limiter has never run. Not once.
**FACT (High). Discovered while fixing the CSRF hole; empirically verified, and it retracts a strength I credited above.**

In Elysia, a lifecycle hook registered inside a **named** plugin defaults to **local** scope — it applies only to routes defined on that plugin instance. `securityPlugin` (`security.plugin.ts:90`) defines no routes, and its rate-limit hook at `:136` carries no `as` option. Every application route is registered on the parent app. **Therefore the hook never fires.**

Minimal reproduction against the installed Elysia 1.4.29:
```
local-scoped  onBeforeHandle fired: 0   ← the rate limiter
global-scoped onBeforeHandle fired: 1   ← what it needed
```

**What is consequently unprotected:** the 5-per-15-min login cap, the phone/Google verify caps, the refresh cap, the review/report spam caps, the upload cap, and the global 100/min ceiling. The `X-Forwarded-For` trusted-proxy hardening that a prior audit logged as a fixed Critical is real code that has never executed. **Credential stuffing against `/auth/login` is currently unthrottled at the HTTP layer.**

**Partial mitigations that do work:** the check-in OTP has its own DB-backed 5-strike/15-min lockout in `checkin.service.ts:250-257` (independent of this hook), and `user.model.ts:104-108` tracks failed admin logins. Those are genuine.

**Fix — and do NOT simply add `as: 'global'` and deploy.** Enabling a limiter that has never run in production is its own outage risk: behind a load balancer with `TRUSTED_PROXIES` unset, every request presents the LB's IP, so all users would share a single 100/min bucket and the API would brown out immediately. Sequence it: (1) set `TRUSTED_PROXIES` correctly for the environment, (2) enable the hook in log-only mode and observe real per-IP rates for a few days, (3) tune the caps to observed traffic, (4) enforce. Also fix the unbounded-`Map` OOM vector below in the same change, since both land the moment real traffic hits this code path.
**Priority: Critical to schedule, but must not be a same-day flip.**

### 🔴 HIGH — Admin permission matrix not enforced on KYC verification
**FACT (High).** `AdminPermission.APPROVE_VERIFICATIONS` and `VIEW_VERIFICATIONS` are defined in the enum and mapped to the `VERIFIER` role — and are **referenced nowhere else in the codebase** (verified by exhaustive grep). `verification.routes.ts` guards admin endpoints with `user.role !== UserRole.ADMIN` only (lines 231, 267, 300, 326). **Any admin of any tier — including `ANALYST`, whose entire purpose is read-only analytics — can approve or reject identity KYC.** `admin.routes.ts` and `reports.routes.ts` do this correctly with `requirePermission`, which makes this an oversight rather than a design choice.
**Impact:** privilege escalation within the admin tier; a compromised or malicious analyst account can approve fraudulent identities — the exact trust primitive the platform sells.
**Fix:** add `requirePermission(AdminPermission.APPROVE_VERIFICATIONS)` to the 4 admin verification routes. **~1 hour. Priority: Critical. ROI: enormous** (highest severity-to-effort ratio in the report).

### 🔴 CRITICAL — CSRF is confirmed **exploitable** with `SameSite=None` cookies in production
**FACT (High). Escalated from High to Critical by the verification pass — my first draft left exploitability open; it is now proven.**
`cookies.ts:33` sets `sameSite: 'none'` + `secure: true` in production. Repo-wide grep for `csrf` / `origin` / `referer` / `sec-fetch` checks returns **zero results** — `cors.plugin.ts` only emits response headers, which does not stop a request being *sent*.

The decisive mechanism: Elysia parses `application/x-www-form-urlencoded` via `parseQuery(await request.text())` and `text/plain` via `.text()` — **before** schema validation. Both are CORS-"simple" content types, so no preflight is issued. And several mutating routes carry **no body schema at all**: `gigs.routes.ts:311/337/363/389` (`/publish`, `/close`, `/cancel`, `/complete`) and `auth.routes.ts:457` (`/logout`).

**Failure scenario:** an auto-submitting `<form action="https://api.zts/api/v1/gigs/<id>/cancel" method="POST">` on any website the victim visits **cancels their booked gig** using their cookie. Routes that *do* declare a body schema remain reachable whenever all fields are strings, since urlencoded decodes to a flat string object; only numeric/nested schemas reject.
**Impact:** forced gig cancellation, forced logout, and — once `/complete` is wired to escrow — **forced fund release**.
**Fix:** an `Origin`-header allowlist check in a global `onBeforeHandle` for all non-GET requests. Cheaper and more robust than double-submit tokens given cookies are the sole auth vector.
**Cost:** ~1 dev-day. **Priority: CRITICAL — this is now the highest-severity security finding in the report.** **ROI: very high.**

### 🔴 HIGH — Every admin tier can read raw KYC documents (Aadhaar/PAN/bank scans)
**FACT (High). New — found by the verification pass.** `GET /verification/admin/:id/:type` (`verification.routes.ts:263`) is role-only, and `verification.service.ts:510-637` returns **5-minute presigned S3 URLs to the identity and bank documents themselves.** The careful last-4 masking of *numbers* is therefore irrelevant: an `ANALYST` — a role whose entire purpose is read-only analytics — receives the Aadhaar, PAN, and bank-proof **scans**.
**Impact:** direct DPDP Act exposure; the most sensitive data in the system is readable by the least-privileged admin tier.
**Fix:** same one-line-per-route `requirePermission` as the finding above. **~1 hour, shared fix.** **Priority: Critical.**

### 🔴 HIGH — 42 dependency vulnerabilities (4 critical, 19 high)
**FACT (High). New — `bun audit`, server, deps installed.** See Executive Summary finding 6 for the breakdown. Most urgent: **`elysia` unauthenticated remote ReDoS on the HTTP front door**, and **`mongoose` `$nor` NoSQL-injection** against services that build `$or` filters from user input.
**Fix:** `bun update` in `server/` clears elysia, mongoose, fast-xml-parser and the AWS SDK chain within semver; `firebase-admin` transitives (`protobufjs`, `node-forge`, `websocket-driver`, `@grpc/grpc-js`) need a major bump or `overrides` pins. Then gate it in CI.
**Cost:** ~half a dev-day. **Priority: Critical.** **ROI: enormous.**

### 🟡 MEDIUM — The unenforced-permission bug is a *class*, not one file
**FACT (High). New.** Beyond verification: `reviews.routes.ts:280-291` (`PUT /reviews/:id/admin`) checks `role !== ADMIN` only — `MODERATE_REVIEWS` is never enforced either. Fix all three surfaces together, then grep every `AdminPermission` member for at least one call site.

### 🟡 MEDIUM — Rate-limit map is an OOM vector *once you deploy behind a load balancer*
**FACT (High). New — and it partially undoes the C4 hardening.** `TRUSTED_PROXIES` is mandatory in production (`.env.example:63`). The moment it names your load balancer, `clientIp` is taken from the attacker-controlled `X-Forwarded-For` (`security.plugin.ts:126-128`), and **every distinct forged value mints a new `Map` entry** (`:149`). Cleanup only evicts *expired* entries, and per-route windows run to 15 minutes — so a client rotating XFF accumulates millions of live entries before the first eviction. **Fix:** validate XFF parses as an IP before using it as a key, and cap the map size. Properly fixed by moving to Redis (§16).

### 🔴 HIGH — Gig image upload: no MIME, no size limit, unsanitised filename
**FACT (High).** `gigs.service.ts:806–830`. Route declares `t.File()` with no constraints (`gigs.routes.ts:429`).
- `file.type` is **client-controlled** and passed straight to S3 as `ContentType` → arbitrary content type stored under your bucket.
- `await file.arrayBuffer()` buffers the **entire** upload in memory with no cap; there is no `maxRequestBodySize` in `index.ts`, so **Bun's 128 MB default is the only ceiling** → trivial memory-exhaustion DoS.
- `file.name` is interpolated unsanitised into the S3 key (line 820) → attacker-shaped keys / possible cross-prefix overwrite.

**Two corrections from the verification pass, one narrowing and one widening the finding:**
- **Stored-XSS sub-claim REFUTED.** `s3.service.ts:56` has `ACL: 'public-read'` **commented out**, so objects are private and not directly servable. *But this surfaces a new functional bug:* gig images are stored as **unsigned URLs to a private bucket — so gig images are broken links in the product today.**
- **The fix is far cheaper than I scoped it.** `users.service.ts:340-361` **already implements** exactly the right thing for profile pictures: 5 MB cap, magic-byte detection, UUID key. The gig path just needs to reuse it.
**Cost:** ~2 hours (reuse existing helper), not a day. **Priority: High.** **ROI: high.**

### 🟡 MEDIUM — Regex injection / ReDoS in venue search
**FACT (High).** `venues.service.ts:99`: `filter.city = { $regex: params.city, $options: 'i' }` with **no escaping** — the only unescaped `$regex` in the repo. Every sibling call site correctly uses `escapeRegex` (`gigs.service.ts:572`, which even carries a comment explaining this exact ReDoS; `users.service.ts:257,281`; `admin.service.ts:291-352`).
**Correction from the verification pass:** the route is **not public** — `venues.routes.ts:66` calls `getAuthUser(ctx)`, so this requires authentication. Severity is capped at authenticated DoS: `?city=(a+)+$` pins a CPU core, and **Bun is single-threaded per process, so one request stalls the entire replica.** No data exfiltration. **Fix: 5 minutes** — the `escapeRegex` helper is already imported in three sibling services.

### 🟡 MEDIUM — Arbitrary user-controlled sort field on the admin reports list
**FACT (High). New.** `reports.service.ts:285,292` takes `sortBy` from `t.Optional(t.String())` with **no allowlist**. Sorting on an unindexed field forces an in-memory sort, which Mongo aborts past 32 MB — so `?sortBy=description` is a **one-request 500 against the admin dashboard**. `gigs.service.ts:609-617` gets this right with an explicit sort map; copy that pattern. **~1 hour.**

### 🟡 MEDIUM — Audit logging disabled by default
**FACT (High).** `.env.example`: `ENABLE_ACTIVITY_LOGGING=false`, commented "disabled by default for new platforms." **Admin actions on PII — KYC approvals, bans — are therefore unlogged unless someone flips it.** This is a direct DPDP Act problem (§11) and an incident-response problem. **Fix: default to `true`.** ~1 hour.

### 🟡 MEDIUM — Phone-OTP login is not rate-limited at the route level
**FACT (High).** `DEFAULT_ROUTE_LIMITS` (`security.plugin.ts:40–54`) covers `/auth/login`, `/auth/google/verify`, `/auth/refresh` — but **not `/auth/phone/verify`**, which the last commit added and which `PROJECT_CONTEXT.md` calls "the primary India onboarding path." It falls back to the global 100/min. Firebase enforces its own OTP limits upstream, which mitigates but does not eliminate. **Fix: one line.** **Priority: Medium.**

### 🟡 MEDIUM — GPS check-in is client-reported and therefore not tamper-proof
**FACT (High).** `checkin.service.ts:282–300` compares `dto.location.lat/lng` — supplied by the browser — against the venue. Any user can override geolocation via devtools. Additionally, **artist–organiser collusion is trivial** (organiser reads the OTP over the phone). Legacy gigs with no venue geoPoint **skip the check entirely** with only a `console.warn`.
**Impact:** the "ground truth" framing in `PROJECT_CONTEXT.md` and on the landing page is **stronger than what the code delivers.** Once escrow releases on this signal, spoofing has direct monetary value.
**Recommendation:** keep OTP+GPS as *friction and evidence*, not as proof. Add server-side IP-geolocation cross-check, retain the raw signals for dispute adjudication, and **do not market it as tamper-proof.** Escrow release should additionally require organiser confirmation or a timeout, which the dual-confirm design already provides — *if it is ever reachable.*

### 🟡 MEDIUM — `POST /gigs/:id/complete` bypasses the entire check-in moat
**FACT (High).** `gigs.service.ts:429–448`: the gig owner alone can move `CLOSED → COMPLETED`. No OTP, no artist confirmation. Combined with the scheduler's `BOOKED → CLOSED` downgrade for unconfirmed events, **this is currently the only reachable path to `COMPLETED`.** Harmless today; **the moment escrow attaches to `COMPLETED`, it is a unilateral fund-release lever held by the party who owes the money.**
**Fix:** require artist counter-confirmation, or restrict this route to admin-only dispute resolution. **~1 dev-day. Priority: Critical *before* payments.**

- **Why:** Split settlement and escrow-like hold without needing a Payment Aggregator licence — the single regulatory shortcut that makes this business legal to operate.
- **Business impact:** Enables 100% of revenue. **Nothing else matters until this exists.**
- **Effort:** 6–8 dev-weeks including webhooks, reconciliation, refunds, GST invoicing, TDS 194-O (0.1%).
- **Maintenance:** Medium-high — webhook reliability, idempotency, reconciliation are permanent work.
- **ROI:** Infinite (0 → revenue). **Priority: Critical.**

### 2. Transactional messaging — WhatsApp Business API + email ⭐ ESSENTIAL
- **Why:** The engagement loop cannot close without it. In India, WhatsApp is the notification channel; email is secondary and SMS is a costly fallback.
- **Business impact:** Directly drives bid response rate, outbid re-engagement, and check-in compliance. **Plausibly the single highest-leverage retention intervention available.**
- **Effort:** 2–3 dev-weeks (Gupshup/Interakt/AiSensy BSP + Resend for email). Template approval is a 1–2 week external dependency — **start it now.**
- **Cost:** ~₹0.35/conversation. **ROI: very high. Priority: Critical.**

### 3. Background job queue (BullMQ + Redis) — HIGH
- **Why:** Payment webhooks, payout retries, notification fan-out, and scheduled reminders all need durable retry with idempotency. `setInterval` gives you none of that and breaks on multiple replicas (§9).
- **Business impact:** Prevents lost payouts and duplicate charges — failure modes that cost money and trust directly.
- **Effort:** 1.5 dev-weeks. Redis also fixes the distributed rate limiter. **ROI: high.**

### 4. Search ranking — MEDIUM, and deliberately boring
- **Why:** Results are currently effectively unranked. Sort by a weighted composite of rating × verification × price-fit × responsiveness × distance.
- **Explicitly NOT ML.** With <10k artists and near-zero interaction data, a hand-tuned weighted score will outperform any learned ranker and takes 1 dev-week instead of 12. **Revisit only past ~100k monthly searches.**
- **ROI: medium-high** for the effort.

### 5. Programmatic SEO — MEDIUM-HIGH
- **Why:** §3 shows paid client acquisition is uneconomic. Organic is the only viable demand channel. "Wedding singers in Bandra", "Sufi bands in Pune" — city × genre × event-type pages generated from data you already have.
- **Effort:** 2 dev-weeks for the generator; ongoing content. **ROI: high, slow** (6–12 months). **Start early precisely because it is slow.**

### Explicitly rejected

| Technology | Why not |
|---|---|
| **AI matching / recommendations** | No interaction data to learn from. Deterministic filters + a weighted score solve this better, faster, cheaper, and debuggably. Revisit at ~100k bookings — realistically never for this business |
| **AI chatbot support** | You have no support volume. Solving an imaginary problem |
| **LLM proposal writing** | Actively harmful — homogenises artist pitches, destroying the differentiation buyers use to choose |
| **Event-driven microservices** | 38k LOC and one engineer. A modular monolith is correct |
| **Blockchain / smart-contract escrow** | Razorpay Route does this legally, cheaply, and today |
| **Native mobile apps** | The responsive web app is unvalidated. Do not double the surface before the single one works |

## Hard blockers, in order

1. **In-process rate limiter and scheduler** (`security.plugin.ts:69`, `scheduler.service.ts:29`) — **caps you at one server instance.** ~1.5 dev-weeks to fix. **This is the first hard technical ceiling and it arrives at roughly 1,000 concurrent users.**
2. **No queue** — payment webhooks and payouts need durable retry.
3. **Per-request auth DB lookup** — becomes material past ~500 rps.
4. **KYC review is manual** — throughput is bounded by human reviewers. At 1,000 artists/month that is a full-time role. **This is the operational ceiling and it arrives before the technical one.**
5. **Dispute resolution is entirely unmodelled.** At 5% dispute rate on 1,000 bookings/month, that is 50 disputes — each potentially involving a ruined wedding and ₹25,000. **This will break the company before infrastructure does.**

**The honest read: infrastructure is not the scaling constraint at any horizon this company will plausibly reach. Operations is — specifically KYC review throughput and dispute handling. Both need process design and hiring, not code.**

# 19. Growth Recommendations

1. **Artist referral loop** — artist invites an organiser; both get a commission credit on first booking. **Solves cold-start from the supply side, which is the side you can actually recruit.** ~1.5 dev-weeks. **Highest-ROI growth build.**

6. **Planner accounts with rosters** — the highest-LTV segment, currently homeless. ~3 dev-weeks.

**Retention loops that will exist once §21 P0 ships:** outbid → notification → re-bid (artists); booking → check-in → review → reputation → more bookings (artists); completed event → review prompt → rebooking (clients).
**Retention loops that exist today: none.**

**Network effects:** currently zero, and — importantly — **weak even at scale.** Wedding bookings are local and infrequent; the effect is *city-level*, not global. Mumbai liquidity does not help Delhi. **Plan for city-by-city grinding, not viral compounding.**

# 20. Cost Optimization

Infrastructure is ~6% of revenue at 100 bookings and falls from there (§12). **There is nothing meaningful to optimise on the infra bill, and time spent there is time not spent on revenue.** The real optimisations are in scope and engineering time:

1. **Delete the `Application` model and module** — 470+ LOC, dead path, forces dual cross-rejection everywhere. **Saves permanent maintenance drag.** ~2 days.
2. **Delete or shelve the Venues module** — 350+ LOC, no evident consumer, and it holds the one unescaped-regex vulnerability. ~1 day.
3. **Replace WebSocket bid updates with 10-second polling** — removes 2 gateways, ticket auth, refcounting, and reconnect backoff (~500 LOC across server + client). **Especially compelling because the auction it serves should be removed anyway (§17).** Reconsider WS for messaging later, where it actually earns its complexity.
4. **Defer admin analytics** — currently broken (wrong field names, hardcoded zeros) and unused at this scale.
5. **Fix the N+1 in `getMyBids`** — 51 queries → 1 (§9). ~1 day.
6. **Add CI** — `bun run typecheck && bun test` on every push. **~2 hours; prevents a whole class of regression cost.**
7. **Do not build native apps.** Do not build the ensemble/cross-pitching system. Do not build lessons. **Not yet.**

**Net effect: roughly 1,300 LOC deleted, ~15% less surface to maintain, and one security finding removed by deletion rather than by fixing.**

# Final Verdict

**Do not launch. Do not build more features. Do five things, in this order.**

**0. Spend three days closing the Top 10 Quick Wins (§23) — this is new, and it now comes before everything else.** `bun update` clears an unauthenticated remote ReDoS on your HTTP front door. Two string changes resurrect the entire admin KYC console. One `requirePermission` line stops every admin tier from reading Aadhaar and bank scans. One Origin check closes a *confirmed-exploitable* CSRF that can cancel a user's booked gig from any website. Six literal deletions remove fabricated 4.8/4.9 ratings from the live UI. **None of this is a project; it is three days, and until it is done the platform is accumulating regulatory liability it cannot process and serving trust signals it invented.**

**1. Instrument, then validate by hand (weeks 1–8, ~₹30k, almost no code).**
Install PostHog and Sentry. Then personally recruit 25 artists and broker 5 bookings manually, outside the product. This tests every assumption in §2 — will artists accept a public price competition, will organisers pay 12%, what actually breaks — at a fraction of the cost of testing them in code. **You are currently one week of real data away from knowing whether any of this analysis is right, and you have chosen not to collect it.** That is the most expensive decision in the project.

**2. Wire what you already own (weeks 1–4, ~₹150k).**
The check-in and review backends are built, tested, and unreachable. `checkinApi` is imported by zero pages, is missing `startEvent`, and sends a payload the server rejects. Three dev-weeks converts your entire claimed moat from dead code into a working product. **This is the highest-ROI engineering work available and it is being deferred behind features that do not exist yet.**

**3. Install the register (weeks 4–14, ~₹500k).**
Verified Pro subscription first — it is the only revenue that does not need escrow and it arrives six weeks earlier. Then payments, escrow, and 12% flat commission on Razorpay Route, with integration tests written *before* the money moves, and `POST /gigs/:id/complete` closed so the payer cannot unilaterally trigger release. **Start gateway onboarding — entity, GST, bank — this week; it is a 2–6 week external dependency sitting on your critical path.**

**4. Kill the auction (weeks 14–18, ~₹200k).**
A strict descending auction with a lower-only ratchet is the single most self-defeating decision in the product. It systematically excludes the supply that would make the marketplace credible. Replace it with quote-compare and keep everything else.

## What I would say as an investor

I would not invest today. Not because the architecture is bad — it is better than most of what I see at this stage, and the founder demonstrably acts on critical feedback: the fabricated testimonials and fake Google rating flagged in June are gone, replaced with an honest pre-launch page that even documents *why* the review markup was removed. That is a genuinely good signal about who is building this.

I would not invest because of what the verification pass revealed about the *pattern*. Three separate systems — check-in, reviews, and the admin KYC console — were declared complete in the project's own documentation and **had never been executed even once.** The KYC queue fails on a lowercase-vs-uppercase string. The verification links 404 on a field named `kind` instead of `type`. The check-in client is missing a method and sends a payload the server rejects. Each is a ten-minute bug. Collectively they prove that **the gap here is not engineering capability — the code that exists is good — it is that nothing has ever been run end-to-end by a human being.** That is also why the 42 CVEs, the broken Docker healthcheck, and the untested second build artifact all shipped: there is no CI, and no one has clicked through the product.

And underneath that: **there is no evidence a single human being has ever wanted this product.** No analytics, no users, no waitlist, no bookings, and a repo untouched for 34 days. The gap between this and an investable company is not the payment integration. It is twenty manually-brokered bookings proving artists and organisers will route a real transaction through you and pay for the privilege. **That work costs nothing but time, requires no engineering, and has not been started.**

## What would change my assessment

- **20 real bookings brokered manually, with 10 completed reviews** → I would take this seriously. This is the single highest-value input.
- Check-in and review UI shipped and used → the moat becomes real rather than theoretical
- First ₹1 of revenue from any source → validates willingness to pay
- 100 verified artists in one city → proves supply acquisition works
- Evidence of a repeat buyer (a planner booking three times) → fixes the LTV problem that otherwise caps this business

## What would make me walk away

- Building ensembles, lessons, or native apps before the money layer works
- Launching with the fake earnings page and no notifications
- Another 30 days of no commits
- Treating the OTP GPS check-in as tamper-proof after reading §10

**This project is roughly 12–16 dev-weeks and one deliberate go-to-market push away from being a real, testable business. That is a genuinely good position — most projects at this maturity are much further away. The risk is not that the remaining work is hard. It is that the remaining work is unglamorous, and the temptation to build the ensemble system instead of the payment system is very strong.**
